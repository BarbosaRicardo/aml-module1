Gradient descent

The story

A hiker is dropped into a foggy valley wearing a blindfold and one instruction: get to the lowest point. She cannot see. She can only feel the tilt of the ground under her boots. So she does the only sensible thing, which is to shuffle downhill.

Her stride length is the learning rate. Set it tiny and she is technically descending, at roughly the pace of a glacier, and the semester ends before she arrives. Set it enormous and something stupid happens: she takes a running leap, sails clean over the bottom of the valley, and lands partway up the opposite slope. Which is steeper. So her next leap is bigger. She is now pinballing up the sides of the valley, getting worse with every confident step, which is the single most common way training runs die.

Steepest descent is the same hiker with a budget: before each step she sends a drone along the chosen direction to find exactly where the ground stops falling, and walks precisely that far. Better steps, more expensive steps.

Now switch the terrain to wavy. The valley becomes an egg carton. Our hiker settles smugly into the first dimple she finds and reports success, never knowing there was a canyon two dimples over. She has not failed at her job. Her job was local. That is what non-convex means, and it is why the whole rest of this module exists.

What to try

  • Leave it on the salad bowl and drag η up past 1.6. Watch the steps grow instead of shrink. That is divergence, and notice the loss rising while the algorithm is still doing exactly what it was told.
  • Drop η to 0.03. It behaves perfectly and gets nowhere. Both failures look like "it isn't working," and they need opposite fixes, which is why this one knob eats so much of your time.
  • Switch to the egg carton and try starting points 0.5, then 5.0, then 9.5, with η around 0.3. Same algorithm, same surface, three different answers. Nothing went wrong. Non-convex just means where you start decides where you land.
  • On the egg carton, find a start where the slope is nearly flat but you are nowhere near the bottom. The hiker crawls. That is the oscillation-and-stall problem the slides warn about when the surface is almost flat and the derivative is close to zero.
The formula, now that you have seen it move
θ ← θ − η∇θℒ(θ)
∇ is the tilt she feels. The minus sign is "walk downhill, not up." η is the stride. That is the entire algorithm; everything else in this module is a workaround for one of its failure modes.

Particle swarm optimization

The story

Thirty food critics are airdropped into a city none of them has visited, hunting the single best taco stand. Nobody gets a map. Nobody gets a phone signal strong enough for GPS. What they do get is a group chat.

Each critic wanders, eats, and remembers the best thing they personally have eaten. That is lbest. The chat also tracks the best thing anyone has reported. That is gbest. Every few minutes each critic decides where to walk next by blending three urges: keep going the way I was going (momentum), drift back toward my own best taco, and drift toward whatever the chat is raving about. Then they add a coin flip, because thirty people walking in perfectly straight lines toward the same address is how you find a local optimum and miss the whole east side of town.

Nobody ever computes a derivative. Nobody needs the city's elevation map. They just eat, compare, and gossip. That is why PSO works on surfaces gradient descent chokes on, and why it comes with no guarantee whatsoever: thirty critics can absolutely all end up at a mediocre stand together, having a lovely time, one block from greatness.

What to try

  • Set c₂ to 3 and c₁ to 0. Everyone sprints at whatever the chat found first. The swarm collapses onto one point almost immediately, usually the wrong one. That is pure exploitation.
  • Now flip it: c₁ to 3, c₂ to 0. Nobody listens to anyone. Thirty independent random searches, no collaboration, painfully slow. That is pure exploration.
  • Keep both near 1.4 and run it repeatedly. Sometimes it nails the global minimum, sometimes it doesn't. Same settings, different answer. It is stochastic and greedy, exactly as the slides say.
  • Shrink the swarm to 3. Watch how much worse it gets. More critics means more of the city covered before the gossip converges.
The formula
Vk+1 = Vk + c₁·rand·(lbesti − Xk) + c₂·rand·(gbest − Xk)
Read it as three urges added up: keep moving, go to my best, go to the group's best. rand is the coin flip that keeps the swarm from marching in lockstep. Position then updates as Xk+1 = Xk + Vk+1.

Bayesian optimization

The story

You have inherited a wine cellar with ten thousand bottles and a liver with ten tastings in it. Somewhere down there is the best bottle. Opening one costs you a tenth of everything you have, and you cannot un-open it. Grid search would be opening every hundredth bottle in order until your liver fails. Random search is grabbing blind. Neither is what a sommelier does.

A sommelier opens one, tastes it, and immediately builds a mental map of the whole cellar from that single data point. Not just "this bottle was an 8." More like: "so the north wall is good, and things near it are probably good, and I have absolutely no idea about the back corner." That map is the Gaussian process: a best guess everywhere, plus an honest confession of how unsure it is everywhere. The shaded band on the chart is the confession.

Then comes the interesting decision. Bottle A the map says is probably an 8. Bottle B the map has no clue about, could be a 2, could be a 12. Which do you open? The acquisition function is your answer to that, written down as arithmetic. Expected Improvement asks: "how much better than my current best could this realistically be?" and it answers high both for promising bottles and for genuinely mysterious ones. That is the same exploration-versus-exploitation trade the swarm was making, except now it's deliberate instead of emergent.

Watch the band on the chart. Every time you sample, it pinches shut at that point, because you know that spot now. The algorithm hunts the wide parts.

What to try

  • Hit "open the next bottle" one at a time and watch where it chooses. Early on it jumps into wide, unexplored bands. Later it fusses around the best region it has found. Nobody programmed that switch; it falls out of the arithmetic.
  • Watch the grey band collapse to nothing at every sampled point. Uncertainty is not vague hand-waving here, it is a number the algorithm computes and then attacks.
  • Crank ξ to 0.5 and restock. Now it is obsessed with the unknown and wanders more before settling. Drop it to 0 and it becomes greedy, drilling into the first good region it finds.
  • Notice how few samples it needs. That is the entire selling point: when each evaluation means retraining a model for six hours, ten smart evaluations beat a thousand dumb ones.
The formula
f(θ) ∼ 𝒜𝑵(m(θ), k(θ,θ′)) — the mental map: a mean guess and a covariance kernel saying how much nearby bottles resemble each other.

αEI(θ) = 𝔼[max(fbest − f(θ), 0)] — expected improvement. The max(·, 0) is the important bit: a bottle worse than your current best scores zero, not negative. There is no penalty for a disappointing gamble, so genuine unknowns stay attractive.

Early stopping

The story

A student is editing an essay. Draft 3 is better than draft 1. Draft 7 is better than draft 3. Around draft 15 something turns: they start "fixing" things that were fine, chasing a private sense of polish that has drifted away from anything a reader would notice. By draft 40 the essay is a personal artifact. It is optimal for an audience of exactly one person, who has now read it forty times and can no longer tell.

That is overfitting, and here is the cruel part: the student's own sense of quality improves monotonically the entire time. Training loss goes down forever. It has to. It is measuring how well you fit the thing you've been staring at. The only way to catch the turn is a friend who reads each draft cold, and that friend is the validation set. When their opinion stops improving, stop writing.

But friends are noisy. One bad review might just be a bad mood. So you set a patience: the number of consecutive disappointing drafts you'll tolerate before you accept the trend and put the pen down. Patience 1 and you quit at the first hiccup, usually way too early. Patience 30 and you have written the private artifact anyway.

Then the move everyone forgets: you don't submit the last draft, you submit the best one. The algorithm keeps a saved copy of the weights from the good epoch and restores them. Stopping late is survivable if you rewind. Stopping late and keeping the final weights is not.

Your instructor ties this to the Secretary Problem, where you interview candidates one at a time and can never go back. The optimal strategy there is to look at the first 1/e of them, about 37%, purely to learn what "good" looks like, then hire the next one that beats everything you've seen. Same shape of problem: decide when to stop, with partial information, knowing that both stopping too early and grinding on forever cost you.

What to try

  • Set patience to 1. It almost always fires far too early, tripped by a single noisy epoch. This is why the parameter exists at all.
  • Push patience to 25. It now catches the true minimum reliably, and wastes a pile of compute getting there. That is the whole trade-off in one slider.
  • Drag validation noise to 0. Suddenly patience barely matters, because a clean signal needs no tolerance. Then drag it to 2.5 and watch low-patience runs fall apart.
  • Watch the training curve the entire time. It never turns up. Not once, at any noise level. If you were monitoring training loss you would have no signal at all, forever.
The formula
t* = argmint ℒval(t)
The best epoch is wherever validation loss bottoms out. Early stopping is just a practical, online way to find t* without knowing the future, and patience is the noise tolerance that makes it survive a real validation curve.
